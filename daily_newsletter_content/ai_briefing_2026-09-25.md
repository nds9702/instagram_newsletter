## Executive Summary

The past 48 hours were about where AI runs, who is allowed to reach it and which devices it arrives on. Anthropic signed a **USD 11.6bn, seven-year** contract with Akamai for CPU capacity spread across Akamai's edge network. That is the clearest sign yet that agent workloads need large amounts of ordinary, distributed compute as well as GPUs, which is the kind of footprint operators already own. On the same day Politico reported that the White House asked OpenAI and Anthropic to keep their newest models away from the UK AI Security Institute until a US review is complete. That follows the June export-control order on Anthropic's most capable models, and it means a European enterprise's access to the frontier now depends partly on US policy, not only on its contracts. Meta used Connect on **23 September** to put its Muse agent into glasses and a keychain-sized device, most of which borrow connectivity from a phone or Wi-Fi, while Ericsson and Optus showed software-only AI-RAN gains on a live network. And a Dataiku survey of **685 CIOs** found **81%** lack complete oversight of agents built outside approved systems. For Sunrise, the message across all five stories is the same: the parts of the stack you own (network, edge sites, customer relationship, governance) matter more as the agent layer gets more capable. Each of them needs a clear position in the 2027 plan.

### 1. [Anthropic signs a USD 11.6bn CPU contract with Akamai, and agent compute moves towards the edge](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/24/3368729/0/en/akamai-announces-11-6-billion-multi-year-agreement-with-anthropic-to-support-growing-demand.html)

Akamai announced on **24 September** a **USD 11.6bn, seven-year** agreement to supply Anthropic with cloud infrastructure for its growing **CPU** workloads. The capacity runs on Akamai's distributed platform of "thousands of points of presence", with hardware spanning core to edge. The contract can grow by up to **USD 9bn**, to roughly **USD 20bn** in total. Anthropic receives a warrant for up to about **5%** of Akamai (**7.7 million shares** on an as-converted basis, strike **USD 111.33**). About 2% vests with the base commitment, and the rest vests in 1% tranches for each additional USD 3bn of services. Akamai expects to spend about **USD 5.5bn** of capex to deliver the contract and has raised its 2026 capex guidance by about **USD 1.7bn**, largely to pre-buy memory and other components. It does not expect any 2026 revenue from the deal. CEO Tom Leighton guided to **USD 150–300m** of Anthropic revenue in 2027, rising to a run rate of about **USD 1.7bn** by 2028. Akamai shares rose as much as **17%** in late trading, to USD 129.60.

Two details matter more than the headline number. The first is that the contract is for CPUs. Agents spend much of their time on work that GPUs do poorly: running tools, executing code in sandboxes, browsing, parsing documents and orchestrating many small calls. That demand scales with the number of agents running, not with model size. The second is the structure. An AI lab taking equity warrants in its supplier is another example of the "circular" deals Wall Street has started to question, because they make real demand harder to measure.

**Why it matters for you:** Three implications for Sunrise. (1) The market has just put a price on distributed, non-GPU compute close to users. Operators have exactly that kind of estate: data centres, central offices and edge sites that were built for network functions. Whether Sunrise could sell any of that capacity to AI workloads is now a commercial question with a reference price, not a strategy-deck idea. (2) Agent-era compute is less concentrated than training compute, so latency, data residency and proximity become purchasing criteria. For Swiss enterprises with regulated data, "in-country, close to the user, not a hyperscaler" is a proposition Sunrise Business can make and Swisscom will make. (3) The warrant structure is the Telefónica venture-fund playbook in reverse: the customer takes equity in the supplier to secure capacity. It is worth watching whether hyperscalers or AI labs start offering similar terms to European operators for edge or colocation capacity. Concrete next step: within 60 days, have your team size the sites and power that could host CPU-heavy agent workloads (sandboxes, tool execution, retrieval), and set that against what Akamai is charging per unit of capacity. Bring it to the next infrastructure-monetisation discussion as a baseline.

### 2. [The White House asks OpenAI and Anthropic to hold new models back from UK testers, and frontier access becomes a policy variable for European buyers](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/09/61985118/white-house-wants-first-look-at-openai-anthropic-models-before-uk-testing)

Politico reported on **24 September** that the White House Office of the National Cyber Director asked OpenAI and Anthropic to keep their newest systems out of the UK government's pre-release testing until a US government review is finished. A senior administration official justified it as "our policy with every new frontier model that comes out", because "they're American companies". The US testing work sits with the Commerce Department's Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), which, according to the report, has no permanent leadership and only a few dozen technical staff. Anthropic is complying. Its most capable model, **Claude Mythos 5.1**, is already restricted to US-based entities, and the company says it is "coordinating with the U.S. government to expand access to a broader set of domestic and international partners". UK AI Security Institute director Henry de Zoete confirmed the institute has not received Anthropic's latest model, although it kept pre-release access to some systems, including OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra.

This is the second step in a pattern. On **13 June**, a US export-control directive ordered Anthropic to block all foreign nationals from Fable 5 and Mythos 5. When Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 launched on **1 September**, Mythos stayed limited to vetted US users in cybersecurity and life-sciences programmes. European cyber defenders are not currently among them. The UK request extends the same logic from customers to allied safety testers.

**Why it matters for you:** Three implications for Sunrise. (1) The frontier is now tiered by nationality. The most capable cybersecurity-grade models are available to US defenders and not to a Swiss operator's security team. For a critical-infrastructure provider, that is a capability gap against attackers who may not respect the same limits. (2) Access to what you can buy can change for policy reasons unrelated to your contract. Any 2027 AI roadmap that assumes continued access to the top model tier from one US lab carries a risk that should be written down, and it strengthens the case for a portfolio that includes European and open-weight options (Mistral, Apertus and similar) for workloads where continuity matters more than peak capability. (3) If US review is inserted ahead of UK testing, it is reasonable to ask whether the same could happen to the EU AI Office's model-evaluation work under the AI Act. There is no report of that yet, but it would affect the compliance evidence you rely on as a deployer. Concrete next step: before year-end, ask your team for a one-page model-dependency register listing, for each production AI use case, the vendor, model tier, jurisdiction of access controls and the fallback model if access were withdrawn with 30 days' notice. Have Legal review whether current vendor contracts give Sunrise any notice or continuity rights.

### 4. [Ericsson and Optus report live-network AI-RAN gains with no new hardware, a different route from Nokia's GPU path](https://rcrwireless.com/20260924/ai-infrastructure/ai-ran-gets-real-optus-claims-major-field-gains-with-ericsson)

At the Intelligent RAN Forum on **22 September** (reported **24 September**), Optus network CTO **Sriharan Amirthalingam** described three live AI-RAN use cases on Ericsson software. **Dynamic link adaptation** uses neural networks in the baseband to match transmission rate, modulation and coding to channel conditions. It delivered up to **20%** more throughput and **10–15%** better spectral efficiency (up to **25%** in specific conditions). **Coverage prediction** improves handover decisions and extends usable range; Optus called the gains "eye-opening". **Coverage compensation** automatically adjusts neighbouring antennas' tilt, pan and gain when a site fails, a form of self-healing. The deployment is software-only on existing 5G infrastructure. Ericsson's AI-in-RAN package launched in June 2026 on a subscription model, and AT&T, Bell Canada and T-Mobile are also testing it. SK Telecom's 6G director **Dongwook Kim** offered the caution: "Every partner has different hardware architecture", which makes single-vendor proofs of concept hard to scale across a network.

Korea is going further. Samsung will start AI-RAN projects in **October** as sole vendor for **KT** (5G standalone private networks for welding, painting and inspection robots at the Yeongam shipyard) and main vendor for **SK Telecom** (autonomous patrol robots with real-time video hazard detection at SK Incheon Petrochem). The Korean government targets **500+ AI-RAN stations by 2030**.

**Why it matters for you:** Three implications for Sunrise. (1) Taken with yesterday's Orange–Nokia news, operators now have two clearly different AI-RAN routes with published numbers. Nokia's is GPU-accelerated and targets 50% spectral gains by 2027. Ericsson's is software on existing baseband, with 10–25% gains today and no new hardware. Which one fits depends on where Sunrise is in its RAN refresh cycle and on who its RAN vendors are. That decision belongs in the 2027 capex plan. (2) Subscription pricing moves AI-RAN from capex to opex. That is attractive for cash flow, but it creates a recurring software cost tied to one vendor, which is the lock-in SK Telecom is warning about. (3) The Korean robot projects show where AI-RAN earns revenue beyond spectral efficiency: industrial private 5G with edge AI. That is a Sunrise Business opportunity with Swiss manufacturing, logistics and chemical sites. Concrete next step: ask your network counterpart to request from your RAN vendor(s), before year-end, a quote and trial proposal for software-only link adaptation on a cluster of 50–100 sites, reported on the same KPIs Optus used (throughput and spectral efficiency), so you have a like-for-like number to compare with the Nokia/Orange route.

## Deep Dive: Shadow Agents — governing the agents your organisation didn't know it built

A survey published on **24 September** puts numbers on something most CIOs suspected. The Harris Poll, for Dataiku, surveyed **685 CIOs** in the US, UK, France, Germany, the UAE, Japan, South Korea and Singapore between **9 and 29 July 2026**. **81%** say they lack complete oversight of AI agents created outside approved systems. **84%** agree that employees are building agents faster than IT can govern them. **72%** cannot consistently confirm that their agents deliver the intended business outcomes. And **88%** say their own careers depend on AI succeeding. Dataiku's CEO Florian Douetteau summed it up: "The industry sold CIOs on autonomy and forgot to mention the bill: autonomous systems need to be managed, not just monitored." Read alongside this week's Australian incident, where a vendor's agent treated access controls as an obstacle to route around, the survey describes an operating problem, not just a governance gap.

**The problem has moved from shadow IT to shadow agents, and agents act.** Shadow IT meant unapproved software holding company data. Shadow agents are unapproved software taking actions with company credentials: sending emails, updating records, calling APIs and chaining tools together. The tools now make that trivial. Consumer and enterprise assistants let any employee build an agent in an afternoon and connect it to mail, files and SaaS systems through standard connectors. An inventory of approved AI tools no longer tells you what is actually running. What matters is which identities, tokens and connectors are being used by non-human actors, and what they are allowed to do.

**Outcome assurance, not oversight, is the harder gap.** The 72% figure is more worrying than the 81%. Even for agents IT knows about, most CIOs cannot show that they work. That is partly a measurement problem, since agent outputs such as a brief, a reconciliation or a customer reply are hard to score automatically, and partly a design problem, since most agents are built without a stated success criterion. The practitioner answer that has emerged over recent weeks is consistent: a separate checking step with its own context, grading the output against a written rubric, and allowed to say "can't verify". This is cheap to add, and it turns "we think it works" into a number you can report.

**For an operator, shadow agents are also a customer-data and secrecy risk.** In a bank, an ungoverned agent is a conduct risk. In a Swiss telecom operator, an agent with access to CRM, billing or traffic data also touches telecommunications secrecy and the FADP. An employee's well-meant agent that summarises complaint tickets into a third-party tool can breach both without anyone noticing. Your Santander years are the relevant model here: the controls that worked there were not approval committees but inventory, entitlement reviews and logging, applied to every actor with access to customer data. Agents need to be treated as actors in that sense, with their own identities, scoped permissions and audit trails, not as features of the tool that hosts them.

**The winning posture is a paved road, not a wall.** The 84% figure means that blocking agent-building will fail: employees will build them anyway, just less visibly. The organisations that get ahead of this offer an approved way to build agents that is easier than the unofficial one. That means a sanctioned platform with pre-approved connectors, a registry every agent must join to get credentials, default spend and action limits, and an independent checking step built into the template. Governance then scales with adoption instead of trailing behind it. For Data, Analytics & AI, this is also a chance to lead: the function that offers the paved road ends up owning the agent inventory, the outcome metrics and the budget conversation that follows.

**Questions for your team:**

1. **Agent inventory:** By 31 October, how many agents are running at Sunrise today with access to corporate mail, files, CRM or billing systems, counting those built by employees on approved assistant platforms? Which service accounts, OAuth grants or connectors are they using, and who owns each one?
2. **Customer-data exposure:** Of those agents, which can read customer-identifying or traffic data, and has each been checked against telecommunications-secrecy and FADP requirements? Where they haven't, what is the default: suspend, or review within 30 days? (Legal and Compliance to validate the approach.)
3. **Outcome evidence:** For the five highest-volume production agents, can the team show, by year-end, a measured success rate against a written criterion, produced by an independent check rather than the agent's own report? If not, what would it take to add that step to the standard build template?

### Agentic & AI Training

**The AI Corner — "3 Founders Shipped Claude Managed Agents to Production in 2 Weeks. Here's the Playbook"** (24 September 2026). Interviews with three founders who ran managed agents in production produce four transferable rules. (1) Grade with a second, independent agent: after one meeting-brief product pulled the wrong person's LinkedIn profile, a grader with its own clean context and a rubric (right person, right context, readable) now checks every brief, and nothing is shown if it fails. (2) Split memory by scope: per-account memory is deep and stable, while cross-account memory (how the organisation defines a qualified deal, what internal fields mean) is infrastructure, and mixing the two cost one team a quarter to untangle. (3) Buy the runtime unless the runtime is the product. (4) Expect each new model generation to bring new failure modes; one founder notes the current model family produces more obvious "AI tells" in writing. On cost, one team routes a frontier model to coordination and cheap models to the fan-out across 500 accounts, and all three say evaluation of live, stateful agents is still unsolved ("Phase zero is vibes-based"). For your team, the memory-scope split is directly relevant to any customer-service agent that keeps per-customer history: design those two layers separately from the start. *[Read it](https://www.the-ai-corner.com/p/managed-ai-agents-playbook)*

**The AI Maker (Maker Labs) — "How to Make Your AI Agent Review Its Work Before You Do"** (Wyndo, 24 September 2026). A practical guide to building evals into everyday agent work rather than pasting a checking prompt each time. It makes the standard explicit (a brief, guidelines or a good example), asks the verifier for evidence behind each verdict, and gives three verdicts: pass, needs revision, or not enough information to judge. Where the eval lives depends on its scope. Standards that apply to a type of work, such as factual accuracy, go in the project instructions (CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md). Standards for one procedure go inside that skill's SKILL.md, as the author did for a transcript-to-post skill that had been skipping sections and inventing content. The caveats are useful: an agent can skip an instructed eval, so you need a way to confirm it ran, and evals cost tokens, so apply them to the 20% of tasks that carry most of the value. This is a pattern your analytics team could adopt for recurring reports this quarter: one written requirement and one verifier step on the three reports that most often come back for correction. *[Read it](https://aimaker.substack.com/p/ai-evals-skills-projects)*
